## 2. One-paragraph summary

An embedding is a fixed-length vector produced by pooling a transformer's token vectors — "whether you are embedding five tokens or 5,000 tokens the output is going to be exactly the same" (Prompt Engineering, 2024-10), the compression "is inherently lossy" (Unstructured, 2024-07) and the result is "very very coarse grain topic driven" (Reimers, 2025-05): it finds *what a passage is about*, not a date, a negation, an exact identifier or a table cell. Chunking exists to hand the embedding model a unit it can represent and the generator a unit it can use — Kamradt's commandment: "your goal is not to chunk for chunking sake… [but] to get our data in a format where it can be retrieved for value later" (2024-01). The market converges on a **baseline** — a recursive splitter at roughly 200–400 tokens of the embedding model's tokenizer, no overlap, which Chroma's token-level evaluation found "consistently high performing across all evaluation metrics" while the OpenAI Assistants default of 800 tokens with 400 overlap had "slightly below-average recall and the lowest scores across all other metrics" (2024-07) — and on **page or element boundaries** when the document has them ("page level chunking… is honestly a pretty strong baseline", Jerry Liu 2024; Reimers 2025; Unstructured's by-title and standalone tables). Above the baseline, three families recover the context a chunk loses: **contextual retrieval** (an LLM prepends 50–100 tokens of document-situating context before embedding; Anthropic measured the top-20 retrieval failure rate falling 35 % alone, 49 % with contextual BM25, 67 % with reranking, at "$1.02 per million document tokens" with prompt caching), **late chunking** (embed the whole document with a long-context, mean-pooling model and pool per chunk afterwards — naive-chunking storage, gains that grow with document length — Jina; Weaviate) and **multi-representation indexing** (embed a summary, hypothetical questions or sentences and return the page or document — Kamradt; Martin; Liu 2024). The model is chosen by **shortlisting on MTEB and deciding on your own corpus** — "find the benchmark that look closest to your use case", then restrict by size, language, context, quantization and latency (Radu, 2026-08); benchmarks lose predictive power as the field overfits them (Reimers 2022; MTEB's abstract: "no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks") — and **pinned with its version, dimensions, dtype, metric and `input_type` convention**: queries and documents get different prefixes (Voyage; Cohere), dimensions can be cut only on a Matryoshka-trained model (OpenAI; Voyage; Cohere), vectors can be quantized to int8 or one bit at a measured loss (Radu), and a model change means re-embedding everything ("you have no way around it… reindex everything", Glean 2025). The evaluation that makes this decidable is cheap and deterministic: generate questions from chunks, check the chunk comes back, compute token-level recall, precision and intersection-over-union (Chroma; Liu: "why guess when we can test", "tests that are really fast really cheap"). Fine-tuning the embedding model is "a much more constrained problem" than fine-tuning an LLM (Husain) and waits for real query–document pairs; multi-vector and vision-RAG buy fidelity on visual documents at known storage and token costs — text where the parse is good, pixels where it is not (Reimers 2025).

## 3. Detailed digest

### 3.1 What an embedding is, and what it is not

**The object.** Lance Martin (2024-04): documents are split "because embedding models actually have limited context windows… on the order of maybe 512 tokens up to 8,000 tokens or beyond", "each document is compressed into a vector and that vector captures a semantic meaning", questions are embedded "in the exactly same way". Reimers (2022) on the **dense bi-encoder**: a transformer yields contextualised token vectors, "a pooling operation, for example you take the mean", gives one fixed-size vector. Unstructured names the pooling variants (CLS, mean, max) and the consequence: "This compression is inherently lossy. With larger chunks, the representation may become overly coarse". Training explains the geometry: "you have certain pairs, certain positive pairs… and then you train them to push these positive pairs close together… and all other points should be distant" (Reimers).

**What it is not** — four speakers who do not cite each other. Reimers (2025-05), demonstrating "just embeddings": "which products did Jenkins buy from us" returns invoices for "Cox Moore… Hinton and Atkins"; "list me all invoices from 2016" returns 2018, 2017, 2021; "what's the GDP of Germany" returns "GDP, GDP rank, GDP growth, GDP per capita" about India, China, the United States — "embedding models are like very very coarse grain topic driven". Jason Liu (TWIML): "I love coffee and I hate coffee are they similar or dissimilar? They could be similar on a dating app… but maybe they're dissimilar… it's really a big assumption to think that we know what is and is not similar in this embedding space". Anthropic: a query for "Error code TS-999" — "an embedding model might find content about error codes in general, but could miss the exact 'TS-999' match". Liu's blog (2024-05): "what is the latest, blah, blah, blah… fundamentally does not embed anything and you need to end up using date filters". Exact identifiers, dates, ranges, negations and table cells are handled by **lexical search, metadata filters and structure** — session 10, noted here as the boundary of session 7.

**Two vendor facts for the config.** OpenAI's v3 models "lack knowledge of events that occurred after September 2021" — a corpus whose vocabulary is newer is the out-of-domain case of §3.6. OpenAI embeddings "are normalized to length 1", so cosine "can be computed slightly faster using just a dot product" and cosine and Euclidean "will result in the identical rankings"; "the choice of distance function typically doesn't matter much" (Radu: dot product on normalised vectors, Hamming on bits).

### 3.2 Chunking — why, the commandment, the baseline and the levels

**Why, and the commandment.** Chunks exist for the embedding model's maximum input ("expressed in tokens, not characters or words" — Unstructured), for representation quality ("like trying to describe a book by averaging all its chapters" — Weaviate) and for the generator's attention (Kamradt quoting Anton Troynikov: distracting context "does tend to measurably destroy the performance of the overall application"). The human test two vendors state identically: "if the chunk of text makes sense without the surrounding context to a human, it will make sense to the language model as well" (Pinecone; Weaviate). Kamradt: "rather than starting with hey how should I chunk my data your question should really be what's the optimal way for me to pass the data that my language model needs for my task". Weaviate's gate comes first: "Does my data need chunking at all?… FAQs, product descriptions, or social media posts, you usually do not need to chunk them. Chunking can even cause problems."

**Levels 1–3.** *Fixed-size*: "extremely simple… very rigid… I don't know anybody that does this in production" (Kamradt). *Overlap*: the tail of one chunk repeated at the head of the next, "between 10 % and 20 %" (Weaviate) — §3.3 for what the measurement says. *Tokens, not characters*: count with the model's tokenizer (OpenAI: "for third-generation embedding models… use the `cl100k_base` encoding"; Ebbelaar wraps the OpenAI tokenizer so Docling counts the model's tokens). *Recursive*: a prioritised separator list — paragraphs, lines, sentences, words, characters — applied to anything still too long; "if I'm starting a project this is my go-to splitter that I use each time" (Kamradt); "usually a solid default choice" (Weaviate); "a great middle ground" (Pinecone). Chroma found LangChain's default separators `["\n\n", "\n", " ", ""]` "would commonly result in very short chunks, which performed poorly" and added `".", "?", "!"`. *Document-aware*: Markdown headers, code by class and function, HTML by tag, LaTeX by section (Kamradt; Weaviate; Pinecone); Kamradt's 2024 go-to of "2,000 4,000 5,000 6,000" characters is dated opinion superseded by §3.3.

**Element-aware chunking** (s6 reuse, now with rules). After parsing, chunking is a *recombination of elements*. Unstructured: "combine two or more consecutive text elements into each chunk that fits"; "Table elements are always treated as standalone chunks. If a table is too large, the table is chunked by rows"; *by title*: "A single chunk should not contain text that occurred in two different sections"; *by page*: the same on page breaks; *by similarity*: a sentence-transformers model with a 0.5 default threshold; overlap on whole elements: "Use with caution as this can introduce noise". Ebbelaar on Docling's **hybrid chunker**: "split chunks that are too large for the embedding model… stitch together chunks that are too small… and it works with your specific tokenizer". **Page-level**: "honestly a pretty strong baseline… Stop worrying about tuning your chunk size of like 512 or 1024" (Liu 2024); "humans… spend a lot of time to create like a nice PDF to adhere to page boundaries" (Reimers 2025). Tables: Abraham's natural-language view for the embedding model (`16` §3.5); Liu (TWIML): "if you see a table we have to save the entire table somewhere else as a separate index". Metadata in the chunk text: "include file and document metadata as additional text in each chunk… File path, Document title, Author, Creation date, Tags" (Liu's blog); a "Modified by" token alone served "10 % of our question base" (TWIML).

**Levels 4–5: semantic, LLM, agentic.** Kamradt's semantic chunker embeds neighbouring sentence groups and cuts where the distance to the next group is in the top 5 % ("breakpoint percentile threshold… 95") — "experimental", "more expensive… slower". Chroma: the percentile "is a relative metric and can lead to larger chunks in bigger corpora", the splitter "is greedy"; their **KamradtModifiedChunker** adds "a binary search over discontinuity thresholds such that the largest chunk is shorter than the specified length"; their **ClusterSemanticChunker** embeds 50-token pieces and uses dynamic programming "to maximize the pairwise cosine similarity between all pairs of pieces within any chunk" — at a cost: "requiring chunks to be re-computed as data is added". Kamradt's agentic chunker extracts **propositions** ("Greg went to the park. Greg likes walking" instead of "he likes walking") and lets an agent-like loop assign each to a chunk with a running title and summary — "slow and… expensive but if you bet that language models will speed up and they'll get cheaper… this type of method starts to come into play". Chroma's **LLMSemanticChunker** avoids rewriting ("repeat the corpus with a `<|split|>` token was costly, slow, and suffered from hallucinations") by tagging 50-token pieces and asking for `split_after: 3, 5`, at "tens of minutes" per corpus. Weaviate's *agentic chunking* (an agent picks the strategy per document) "use[s] a lot of computing power"; Chroma found "no clear signal" in next-token entropy or attention values.

**Other named strategies.** *Hierarchical / parent-document*: embed small, return the parent (LlamaIndex's `HierarchicalNodeParser` 2048/512/128 in Kamradt's demo); Pinecone's **chunk expansion**: "retrieving neighboring chunks within a window… expanded to paragraphs, pages, or even whole documents". *Adaptive* (Weaviate; no evidence) and *post-chunking* at query time (Weaviate's Elysia — vendor feature) are named, not measured. Pinecone's default — "Fixed-sized chunking will be the best path in most cases… the max context window size of the embedding model" — is the position Chroma's measurement contradicts (§5).

### 3.3 Evaluating a chunker — Chroma's token-level method and what it found

**Why document-level metrics cannot see chunking.** Chroma: "benchmarks like MTEB… evaluated with respect to the relevance of entire documents, rather than at the level of passages or tokens, meaning they cannot take chunking into account"; "nDCG@K… is less useful in the context of RAG because the rank order of retrieved documents is less important". Radu: for RAG "your precision is going to be more important, because if you feed the LLM junk, you're going to get hallucination"; nDCG "has this blind spot" — unjudged results count as zero, so "every time you change your relevance function, make sure you update all the documents that you compute NDCG off".

**The method.** Generate from *your* corpus: "we sample from an LLM to generate a query relevant to the documents, as well as excerpts from the corpus… We accept only excerpts which have full-text matches within the corpus as valid"; previous queries in the prompt against duplicates; no compound questions (the model must include an "oath" not to use "and" — "GPT would not comply until we required it"); near-duplicates and off-topic excerpts filtered by cosine thresholds. Cost "approximately $0.01" per question, "1,000 questions would cost around $10"; the concrete set is 472 queries over five corpora, 328,208 tokens. Metrics at the **token level**: recall, precision, **Precision_Ω** ("precision for the case that all chunks containing excerpt tokens are successfully retrieved… an upper bound on token efficiency") and **IoU** — Jaccard with "each t_e among t_r only once" in the numerator and "all retrieved tokens in |t_r| in the denominator", so overlap is penalised; "we can think of text chunks as bounding boxes". Code is public, which is why §7.5 reproduces the metrics on a fixture.

**What it found** (`text-embedding-3-large`, five chunks retrieved, means over corpora; standard deviations ± 25–35 points on recall, so directions, not decimals). Recursive 800/400: recall 85.4, precision 1.5, IoU 1.5. Recursive 400/0: 89.5 / 3.6 / 3.6. **Recursive 200/0: 88.1 / 7.0 / 6.9, Precision_Ω 29.9** — "consistently high performing across all evaluation metrics". **Cluster 400: recall 91.3**; **Cluster 200: precision 8.0, Precision_Ω 34.0, IoU 8.0** (all highest). **LLM chunker: recall 91.9** with average efficiency. Headline: "up to 9 % in recall" between strategies. Conclusions: "reducing chunk overlap improves IoU scores"; 200–400 tokens beat larger (Lucek's reading); the OpenAI Assistants default "of 800 tokens with an overlap of 400 tokens… results in slightly below-average recall and the lowest scores across all other metrics" (Lucek: "it probably won't be anymore"). One counter-case: with the small `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, recall fell from 82.4 to 77.1 without overlap — "for smaller context, overlapping chunks are necessary". The report's own limits: synthetic questions "tend to generate a specific style", a "relatively small" dataset, no timing.

**The practitioner's version.** Liu (TWIML): "given a text chunk can I generate a synthetic question… and then let me check whether or not the question I just generated finds the text chunk"; the number says whether the problem is hard — Paul Graham essays "96 and 97 % recall… the problem is too easy", GitHub issues "60 % recall" because "how to get started" needs a repository filter. Glean "unit test[s] our models" — "a good semantic search model should be really good at paraphrasing" — one set per behaviour; its caveat on synthetic questions: an LLM given a sentence "will just add like question words to the beginning… you don't want to learn just like exact lexical overlap". The cost rule (Liu): "I want tests that are really fast really cheap… every 10 20 minutes", not a judge.

### 3.4 Query vs document representation — `input_type` and one model for both sides

"Questions and documents are very different text objects… documents can be like very large chunks… whereas questions are short, potentially ill worded" (Martin). The vendors fix it at embedding time. **Voyage**: `input_type` is `None`, `query` or `document`; "Voyage automatically prepends a prompt to your inputs before vectorizing them… For query, the prompt is 'Represent the query for retrieving supporting documents: '. For document, the prompt is 'Represent the document for retrieval: '"; "embeddings generated with and without the `input_type` argument are compatible". **Cohere**: `input_type="search_query"` for the query, `"search_document"` for "the text passages that are being searched over", `classification` and `clustering` for those tasks. **OpenAI's** guide exposes no such parameter — one string for both sides. The research behind it is Reimers' 2022 answer to "one model or two": DPR used two, TAS-B one, and "the same model… work[s] totally better especially like for the out of domain setting… this still projects us to the same or roughly the same point in the vector space" for an unseen word. Position: query side and document side are embedded by **one pinned model** with **the provider's conventions applied by the code path, not the caller** (§7.5 Verify 2).

### 3.5 Context recovery — contextual retrieval, late chunking, multi-representation indexing

**The problem.** The SEC-filing chunk "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter… doesn't specify which company it's referring to or the relevant time period" (Anthropic); "its" and "the city" referencing "Berlin… mentioned only in the first sentence" make "What is the population of Berlin?" unanswerable (Jina).

**Contextual retrieval** (Anthropic, 2024-09-19). Prepend "chunk-specific explanatory context to each chunk before embedding ('Contextual Embeddings') and creating the BM25 index ('Contextual BM25')"; the Claude 3 Haiku prompt is printed in full — whole document, the chunk, "Please give a short succinct context to situate this chunk within the overall document for the purposes of improving search retrieval of the chunk" — "usually 50–100 tokens". Measured with "1 minus recall@20": contextual embeddings "reduced the top-20-chunk retrieval failure rate by 35 % (5.7 % → 3.7 %)", with contextual BM25 "by 49 % (5.7 % → 2.9 %)", with a reranker over the top 150 "by 67 % (5.7 % → 1.9 %)" — BM25 and reranking are session 10. Cost with prompt caching, "assuming 800 token chunks, 8k token documents, 50 token context instructions, and 100 tokens of context per chunk… $1.02 per million document tokens" (Alex: "it's basically like studying all of your data before you go and index it"). The chunker stays — "the choice of chunk size, chunk boundary, and chunk overlap can affect retrieval performance" — and "always run evals". What did **not** work: "adding generic document summaries to chunks (we experimented and saw very limited gains), hypothetical document embedding, and summary-based indexing (we evaluated and saw low performance)". Unstructured ships it as a *Contextual Chunker* node after the chunker; the Pinecone build applies it to video frames (a description per 45-second window, image paths as metadata; Alex: "hand-grade your outputs"); Voyage does it inside the model (MongoDB; claim in §4). Reimers on scale: "okay if you do it maybe for a thousand PDFs, but some customers have a billion PDFs and then it becomes yeah very slow, expensive".

**Late chunking** (Jina, 2024-09; Weaviate, 2024-09-05). Abstract: "first embed all tokens of the long text, with chunking applied after the transformer model and just before mean pooling — hence the term late"; README: mean pooling "to smaller segments of this sequence of token vectors", with `jina-embeddings-v2-base-en`'s 8,192 tokens, "roughly ten standard pages". Berlin table: the two sentences that do not say "Berlin" rise from 0.708 and 0.753 similarity to 0.825 and 0.850. BeIR nDCG@10 (256-token chunks, `jina-embeddings-v2-small-en`): from no change (Quora, 62-character documents) to NFCorpus 23.46 → 29.98 — "in all cases, late chunking improved the score… the average length of the documents correlates with greater improvement"; on two datasets "no chunking performs best". Weaviate: two requirements — a long-context model and "a mean pooling strategy… a requirement" — plus chunk-to-token-span mapping; "under 30 lines of code… without any modification to the retrieval pipeline". Storage is the argument against ColBERT: at 8,000 token vectors of 768 float32 dimensions over 100,000 documents, "Late Interaction (no pooling)… ~2.46 TB; Naive Approach… ~4.9 GB; Late Chunking… ~4.9 GB". Caution from both: "limited data available on its performance in benchmarks" (Weaviate); "we will probably need a lot more validation from independent sources" (Prompt Engineering, whose "30 % boost" and "34 GB of VRAM" are §4). Weaviate's "it lessens the requirement for very tailored chunking strategies" is contested in §5.

**Multi-representation indexing.** Kamradt: search "off of something else like a summary… or hypothetical questions" (LangChain's `MultiVectorRetriever`). Martin: "decoupling raw documents and the unit you use for retrieval… you return the full document for the LLM… just get me the right document. It's an easier problem than get me the right chunk". Liu (2024): "embed a lot of different references to that table… an extracted summary, or embed the caption, or all three… and basically link all of them to the same underlying data". **RAPTOR** (Martin): cluster embeddings, summarise each cluster, recurse, index leaves and summaries together for "better semantic coverage across the abstraction hierarchy". Contested against Anthropic's "summary-based indexing… low performance" — §5.

**Long context and the unit of retrieval.** Martin's multi-needle test on a 120,000-token window: retrieval "drops with respect to the number of needles", "gets worse if you ask it to reason", "needles towards the start of the context are harder to retrieve than towards the end" ("recency bias"); "be wary of just context stuffing… there are no retrieval guarantees". His forecast, "document centric RAG": "retrieve full documents rather than worrying about these idiosyncratic parameters like chunk size". Anthropic at the small end: "smaller than 200,000 tokens (about 500 pages of material), you can just include the entire knowledge base in the prompt". None of it removes chunking from a large, changing corpus; it moves the retrieval unit up — why §7.5's decision table starts with "which unit does the generator need?".

### 3.6 Choosing an embedding model — shortlist on benchmarks, decide on your corpus

**Benchmarks lose predictive power** (Reimers, 2022-12, MTEB co-author). "You can't improve what you don't measure", but "the predictive power of a benchmark… decreases… at some point you see some increase on a benchmark but this has like no meaning"; "one benchmark [for] embeddings that has zero predictive power is semantic textual similarity, STS". **BEIR** (2021): "the best dense embedding approach, TAS-B, only was better on 8 out of 18 data sets than lexical search" — "issues for unknown domains", traced to train–test overlap and unseen words; by late 2022 "lexical search is only better on two out of 14 data sets". **MTEB** (abstract): "8 embedding tasks covering a total of 58 datasets and 112 languages… 33 models… no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks". Speed matters: a 6-billion-parameter model "two magnitudes slower" for a small gain — "find a model that's not only good but also which is fast". BEIR's blind spot is this module's: "mainly passage retrieval… 512 word pieces… no… PDF documents with hundreds of pages" — Chroma's criticism three years later.

**The procedure** (Radu, 2026-08-31; Weaviate 2025-01; OpenAI's table). Husain's framing: people "go to a leaderboard and just like pick the best one… or just using an embedding model from the provider that they're using… you're leaving a lot on the table". Radu: "we're going to start by looking at MTEB" — "find the benchmark that look closest to your use case", restrict by size, look for models that "punch way above their weight"; then what MTEB does not cover — quantization of model and vectors, Matryoshka dimensions, query latency and document throughput — and the constraints: "if you need the model to be multilingual, you should choose a multilingual model. If you need this larger context, then you should choose a model that supports that". His start: "from running this on CPU, I would use the int8 quantization. If I run it on GPU, I would use FP16… start with… defaults that don't lose too much and then take it from there… Do we have a performance problem? Cuz you may over optimize… if you have 2 million documents, then a lot of the performance issues… may not apply". Weaviate: domain models for industry terms, general models "will probably work well" for most, closed APIs mean "rate limits and batch processing", open ones hosting, and "I would always recommend running your own benchmarking tests". OpenAI's own table (MTEB score, max input 8192 and pages per dollar for `-3-small`, `-3-large`, `ada-002`) and Voyage's model table (finance, law and code models beside general ones at 32,000 tokens) are the shortlist shape; the numbers live in the practice.

### 3.7 Dimensions and quantization — Matryoshka, int8, binary, and pinning the result

**Dimensions.** More dimensions mean "more space with which to get accuracy… at the cost of larger storage size", and traditionally "you have to re-embed your entire corpus… every time you want to try a different number" (MongoDB). **Matryoshka**: "some models are trained such that the earlier dimensions are more important than the later dimensions. You can basically cut the… vector" (Radu); "they're ordered… I just chop off the last 512" (MongoDB). OpenAI: default 1536 (`-3-small`) or 3072 (`-3-large`); with the `dimensions` parameter "a `text-embedding-3-large` embedding can be shortened to a size of 256 while still outperforming an unshortened `text-embedding-ada-002` embedding with a size of 1536" on MTEB; truncating after the fact "you need to be sure to normalize". Voyage `output_dimension`: 2048, 1024 (default), 512, 256 on the 4- and 3-series; Cohere `embed-v5.0-pro`: `[256, 512, 768, 1024, 1536, 2048]`. Radu saw 512 dimensions beat the full vector on one model and lose "a little bit" on another — "test on your data".

**Quantization, twice.** Model: "int8 is a good quantization to pick" on CPU; on GPU an int8 model "is actually going to be a whole lot slower, but you can run… an FP16 variant" (Radu). Vectors: "if you go to bfloat16, you're probably not going to lose any. So that's basically a no-brainer… all the way down to bit vectors, but then you're going to lose something. Whether that something is worth it or not, that's up to you"; under hybrid search the float-to-bit gap "shrinks a little bit"; at scale "binary with Hamming distance… and then… something more expensive to re-rank" (session 10). Vendor facts: Voyage `output_dtype` `float`, `int8`, `uint8`, `binary`, `ubinary` — binary lists are "1/8 of `output_dimension`"; Cohere `embedding_types` with the same five, "1024 binary embeddings… will become 1024/8 => 128 bytes", with `np.unpackbits` code; MongoDB quantizes "inside the index". The storage side is session 8; what is session 7 is that **dtype and dimension are part of the embedding configuration**, chosen by measurement and recorded with the index.

**Versioning.** Voyage: "All embeddings created with the 4 series are compatible with each other" — stated as a feature because it is not the norm (this KB's reading: a vector is a coordinate in one model's space). Glean: "we do things on a monthly basis… if your model does change… you have no way around it or else the quality of your embedding search is going to become a lot worse over time if you don't reindex everything". Position: **one pinned model id, version, dimension, dtype and metric per index, in a config object and `docs/stack.md`; a change creates a new index and re-embeds everything; two configurations never share an index.**

### 3.8 Fine-tuning, multi-vector, multimodal — when, and what it costs

**Fine-tuning: when.** Husain: "should you fine-tune your LLM?… maybe not… But the embedding is a much more constrained problem, and it's a lot easier to fine-tune the embedding… I always find a really good benefit". Radu: "it's not a big cost… I think anybody should do it". Liu (TWIML) orders the targets: "the first thing to fine tune is likely going to be something like a Cohere reranker… probably for $50 you can get a fine-tuned reranker that outperforms anything off the shelf. I don't know whether fine-tuning embedding models is worth it just because… it's just annoying to own inference, but… that's the second easiest thing". **How (depth parked to s16).** Radu, with Vespa Embed: pairs or triplets, MNR loss ("it can select other documents from the batch as negatives"), hard negatives when it matters, labels from an LLM judge at "0 1 2"; the don't: "take the title and description of the product and train off of that. That did not work. You're supposed to train it based on what is actually going to happen… the real queries" (Husain: "beware of proxies"). **Glean's pipeline**: per-customer BERT-base models, masked-language-model pre-training on the corpus, unsupervised pairs (title–body, anchor, co-access), public data mixed in against "catastrophic forgetting", then query–click pairs; "after 6 months you can see like a 20 % improvement"; monthly retrain and full re-index. Its framing: "for 70 % [of] user questions in the enterprise… by just using very basic signals that you would use on your traditional search engine" — Liu: "80 % of the way there is just full text search" (session 10).

**Multi-vector and late interaction.** Martin on **ColBERT**: "an embedding or vector for every token… for every token in the question you're computing the similarity across all the tokens in the document and you're… taking the max… the final score is… the sum of the max similarities" — "very strong performance, latency is definitely a question". Weaviate: "late interaction performs no pooling step", storage ≈ 500× naive chunking at 8,000 tokens per document. **ColPali** (Qdrant): "extends the late interaction paradigm from text to visual documents… instead of text tokens, it works with image patches… No OCR in pipeline"; version 1.3 "is built on PaLI-3 Gemma 3B" (SigLIP encoder, Gemma 2B, LoRA); "resized to 448 by 448 pixels. Each patch is 14 by 14 pixels… a 32 by 32 grid… That's 1,024 patches in total. Each gets a 128-dimensional embedding vector"; "the same maximum scoring as ColBERT". Radu shows MaxSim as a re-ranking profile, never first phase. Storage and index support are session 8.

**Multimodal: describe-then-embed vs native.** Kamradt (2024) embeds "a text summary of each image" because CLIP's "tech is still not quite there". Liu (TWIML) made the description a measured lever: "if you just say describe this image… recall is like 27 %"; an engineer who hill-climbed "for like a day and a half" reached "a 96 % recall system for finding blueprints" — "it's definitely just feature engineering". Reimers (2025) on why CLIP failed for documents: caption training data, "70 tokens", "224 pixels", and a **modality gap** — "all the image embeddings and all the text embeddings are very very far away"; the mechanism carried is **resolution**: "if you cannot read it, you can't produce an embedding". **Vision-RAG** — embed the page image, retrieve, hand it to a VLM — "makes it so much easier" where "the parsing step breaks" (a Boeing diagram, a multi-header tax table), at stated costs: "one page as a text is about like 500 to like 2,000 tokens. But… as an image… that's 11K image tokens… 10 to 20 times more expensive", "higher rate of hallucination with vision LMs", multi-image synthesis "don't work well yet". His product rule is the position adopted here: "when the PDF is text you want to put it as a text but if the PDF has complex visual elements you want to put it as complex visual element" — `16` §3.4 from the embedding side. Cohere's docs confirm fused text-plus-image inputs for `embed-v5.0-pro`; his remarks on Voyage's model are "speculation" (§4).

## 5. What is contested / trade-offs

- **Fixed-size at the model's maximum vs small recursive chunks.** Pinecone: "Fixed-sized chunking will be the best path in most cases… the max context window size of the embedding model". Chroma: 200–400 recursive tokens, no overlap, best on efficiency at comparable recall. Unstructured "about 250 tokens", Weaviate "512 tokens and a chunk overlap of 50–100 tokens" are two more starting points. **Position:** start at recursive 200–400 tokens of the model's tokenizer with no overlap, or at the element or page boundary when the parser gives one; the token-level eval on your corpus moves it; overlap only where the eval shows recall needs it.
- **Does context recovery make the chunker irrelevant?** Weaviate: late chunking "lessens the requirement for very tailored chunking strategies"; Prompt Engineering: "it doesn't really matter how you chunk". Anthropic: "the choice of chunk size, chunk boundary, and chunk overlap can affect retrieval performance". **Position:** both recover *context*; neither fixes a table split mid-row or a heading separated from its paragraph. Keep the structure-aware chunker; add recovery when the eval shows the gap.
- **Contextual retrieval vs late chunking vs multi-representation.** One LLM call per chunk, any model, helps BM25 too — vs no per-chunk cost and naive storage but a long-context mean-pooling model and documents under its window — vs more vectors and a document store. **Position:** late chunking when the model allows it; contextual retrieval when BM25 must benefit or the corpus is bounded; multi-representation for tables, images and whole-document return. All measured.
- **Summary indexing: three for, one against.** Kamradt, Martin and Liu recommend embedding summaries and returning the parent; Anthropic "evaluated and saw low performance" for summary-based indexing and "very limited gains" from generic summaries prepended to chunks. The units differ (a summary standing for a document vs a shared summary on every chunk). **Position:** summaries find the document; chunk-level context must be chunk-specific.
- **MTEB: start there or stop?** Reimers co-authored it and warns of overfitting; Radu starts with it; Chroma says it cannot see chunking. **Position:** a leaderboard shortlists by task, language and size; nothing else is decided there.
- **Fine-tune or not.** Radu and Husain: "anybody should do it"; Liu: reranker first, "annoying to own inference". **Position:** collect real pairs from day one (the eval set is the training set in waiting); reranker first, embedding model when pairs exist and inference is owned anyway; never on proxies.
- **Text-RAG vs vision-RAG.** Reimers sells vision embeddings and states 10–20× cost and more hallucination; Jerry Liu (s6) says the VLM is a tool. Compatible: pixels where the parse fails.
- **Who benefits.** Every vendor and every consultant with a course. Mechanisms kept with a second source; every benchmark win and every "best" dropped.

### 7.3 New principle — principles/17-embeddings-and-chunking.md (to be created; draft until 2026-11-26; ≤ 28 KB)

1. **The question** (≤ 0.4 KB). When a product retrieves by similarity: how is text turned into a vector and what can that vector *not* represent; into what units is the corpus cut and how is the choice measured; how is the model chosen, configured and pinned; when do context recovery, fine-tuning, multi-vector or vision retrieval earn their cost?
2. **Short answer** (≤ 1.6 KB). An embedding is a pooled, lossy, topic-level representation — exact ids, dates, negation and table cells are lexical and structural problems (s10). Chunk for the generator's unit and the model's limit, from the parsed element tree: recursive 200–400 tokens of the model's tokenizer with no overlap, or the page or element boundary, as baseline; measure with synthetic questions and token-level recall / precision / IoU before changing anything; recover context with late chunking, chunk-specific prefixes or multi-representation indexing when the measurement shows the gap. Shortlist the model on the closest benchmark task, language, max input and cost; decide on your eval set; embed queries and documents with the provider's two conventions through one code path; pin model, version, dimensions, dtype and metric in one config and `docs/stack.md`; a change re-embeds behind a new index. Fine-tune the reranker first, the embedding model when real pairs exist. Multi-vector and vision retrieval buy fidelity on visual documents at known storage and token costs — pixels where the parse breaks. Files: the new practice folder.
3. **Long explanation** — §3.1 *What an embedding is and is not* (≤ 2.5 KB); §3.2 *Chunking* (≤ 5 KB); §3.3 *Evaluating a chunker* (≤ 3.5 KB; decimals in the practice); §3.4 *Query vs document* (≤ 1.5 KB); §3.5 *Context recovery* (≤ 4 KB, including long context and the unit of retrieval); §3.6 *Choosing the model* (≤ 3 KB); §3.7 *Dimensions, quantization, pinning* (≤ 3 KB; vendor limits by pointer); §3.8 *Fine-tuning, multi-vector, multimodal* (≤ 3.5 KB) — each mirrors the same-numbered section here.

### 7.4 Glossary entries to add (`principles/00-glossary.md`)

- **Embedding (dense vector); pooling.** A fixed-length vector from pooling (mean, CLS, max) a transformer's token vectors; lossy, trained on positive pairs, so it encodes topic-level similarity, not exact identifiers, dates or negation. Reimers (2022); Unstructured (2024-07). `17` §3.1.
- **Chunk; chunking commandment.** The unit embedded and retrieved; chosen for the downstream task and the model's limit, never "for chunking's sake". Kamradt (2024-01). `17` §3.2.
- **Token-level retrieval metrics (recall, precision, Precision_Ω, IoU).** Relevance measured on the tokens of verbatim excerpts so a chunker can be evaluated; IoU counts redundant excerpt tokens once in the numerator and all retrieved tokens in the denominator, penalising overlap. Chroma (2024-07-03). `17` §3.3.
- **`input_type` (query vs document embedding).** The provider's convention prepending a different instruction to a query and to a passage; one model for both sides. Voyage; Cohere (read 2026-10-04). `17` §3.4.
- **Contextual retrieval / contextual chunking.** Prepending 50–100 tokens of chunk-specific, LLM-written context to each chunk before embedding and BM25 indexing. Anthropic (2024-09-19). `17` §3.5.
- **Late chunking.** Embedding the whole document with a long-context, mean-pooling model and pooling per chunk *after* the transformer; document context at the storage cost of naive chunking. Jina (2024-09); Weaviate (2024-09-05). `17` §3.5.
- **Matryoshka representation learning (MRL).** Training so the first dimensions carry the most information, letting a vector be truncated (then normalised) without re-embedding. OpenAI, Voyage, Cohere pages (read 2026-10-04). `17` §3.7.
- **Late interaction / multi-vector (ColBERT, ColPali); MaxSim.** One vector per token or image patch, scored by summing over query tokens the maximum similarity to any document vector; higher fidelity, hundreds of times the storage. Martin (2024-04); Qdrant (2026-03); Weaviate (2024-09). `17` §3.8.

**Stack-sensitive points.** Where the tokenizer runs decides how limits are enforced. A pgvector column's dimension is fixed at migration — a guard for free and a migration per re-embed. Rate limits make batch embedding a queued job in request-scoped runtimes. `input_type` is a parameter on Voyage and Cohere and absent on OpenAI — the adapter hides it, the config records it. The harness must run offline (stub embedder) so CI never pays for embeddings to test a chunker.

**Verify (draft contract):**

1. One embedding configuration per index pins model id, version, dimensions, dtype, metric and the provider's query and document conventions, recorded identically in `docs/stack.md` and the index manifest — observer: script — negative: an embed call with no version, an undeclared dimension, or a manifest that disagrees with the config
2. Every query is embedded as a query and every document as a document, by the same model, through one code path — observer: script — negative: an embed call without a side, a query embedded as a document, or a query model differing from the index model
3. Chunks come from a declared chunker (strategy, size and overlap in tokens of the model's tokenizer) recorded in the manifest, and no chunk exceeds the model's maximum input — observer: script — negative: a chunk over the limit, or an index with no chunker record
4. Chunks follow the element tree: a table is one chunk (or row groups with the header repeated) with its embedding-text view, a heading never stands alone or apart from its first paragraph, and each chunk starts with the envelope's metadata prefix — observer: script — negative: a table split mid-row or merged with prose, a lone-heading chunk, or a chunk without its prefix
5. A retrieval eval set exists for the corpus (at least fifty questions with verbatim excerpts, generated and filtered or real) and token-level recall, precision and IoU were computed for the chosen chunker and at least one alternative before the index was built — observer: script — negative: an index built with no eval set, or a chunker recorded with no comparison
6. The embedding model was chosen from at least two candidates filtered by task, language, max input, cutoff and cost and compared on the corpus eval set; the record names the leaderboard only as the shortlist — observer: Martin — negative: a model picked from a rank or from the provider already in use without a comparison — framework: bends
7. A model, version, dimension or dtype change re-embeds the whole corpus behind a new index; no index holds vectors from two configurations — observer: script — negative: an upsert whose fingerprint or dimension differs from the index's
8. Context prefixes, late chunking, quantization below bfloat16, hierarchical indexing and fine-tuning are adopted only with a before/after on the eval set and a recorded cost — observer: Martin — negative: a context prefix or a binary index with no measurement beside it
9. The retrieval eval runs deterministically, with no judge, on every change to the chunker, the configuration or the index, and blocks the merge below the recorded floor — observer: script — negative: a chunker or config change merged without the run
