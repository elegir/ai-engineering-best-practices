## 2. One-paragraph summary

Exact nearest-neighbour search is a scan — "a million times 1,500 computations" (Katz, 2024-01) — and partitioning does not rescue it in high dimension (Liberty, 2021). The answer is an **approximate** index that "does not guarantee that the result will be exact or even the result will be the same for the same underlying data if you build the index multiple times" (Vasnetsov, 2023), and the module is the management of that approximation: **recall against speed against memory against build time**. **HNSW**, a layered proximity graph searched greedily, has three knobs every vendor describes identically — `M` (memory, recall), `efConstruction` (graph quality, build time), `ef` (recall, latency) — and three costs: it lives in RAM, it is expensive to build ("hnsw is all about the build" — Katz), and it **breaks under filters**: past about 1/k of its nodes removed the graph disconnects (percolation theory — Vasnetsov 2019), so post-filtering "is logically broken" (Liberty) and the fixes are filter-aware edges (Qdrant; Weaviate's ACORN variant), a planner that switches to the payload index or a scan when the filter is selective (Qdrant; ClickHouse), or an iterative scan until *k* results pass (pgvector 0.8.0). **Quantization inside the store** — int8 about 4×, binary 32× with Hamming distance "in just two CPU instructions" (Vasnetsov), product 4–64×, rotational at 8, 4 and 1 bit (Weaviate) — is always paired with **oversampling and rescoring against the originals**; Qdrant keeps the originals so it can be undone, Weaviate warns "once set on a collection, quantization can't be disabled". **Storage** is where 2023's unsolved problems — "making it cheap to serve billion vectors" (Vasnetsov), "separation of storage and compute… not solved yet" (Dilocker) — meet Eskildsen's 2026 answer: object storage as "the only stateful dependency", a clustered index because a graph's dependent round trips are "100 milliseconds, 100 milliseconds, 100 milliseconds" on S3, caches warming hot namespaces into NVMe and RAM, at the stated cost of cold queries and writes in the hundreds of milliseconds; Notion reports "a few million dollars annually" saved at "a p50 of 50 to 70 milliseconds" (Mickey Liu, 2025-05). **Multi-tenancy** is store-shaped — a tenant payload index (Qdrant), a flat index per tenant (Weaviate), a namespace that is an S3 prefix (turbopuffer), partitions in Postgres, because "sharing an approximate index between tenants means vectors from one tenant can affect recall (and speed) for other tenants" (pgvector README) — the sentence that refines this KB's RLS rule: on an approximate index the policy is applied *after* the scan, and recall needs an iterative scan or a partition beside it. **The store**: "you could probably get away with PG vector" for a small case already on Postgres (Eskildsen, 2025-11); a search engine "should not be even used as a primary storage… the full update of vectors… due to… a new version of the model… is just a common operation" (Vasnetsov). **Benchmarking**: compare only at equal recall (Qdrant's page; Katz), on your own data, recall@k against exact search with QPS, p99, memory and build time (ANN-Benchmarks README), and monitor recall in production by "sampled brute force comparisons" (Dilocker).

## 3. Detailed digest

### 3.1 Why exact search stops scaling, and when no index is needed

Keen (IBM, 2025-03) frames it: with millions of vectors a scan "would just be too slow", so indexing trades "a small amount of accuracy for pretty big improvements in search speed". Liberty (2021): "a query is a classifier" (dot product and threshold, a cone, a ball); exact evaluation is "n times d", and "any kind of naive partitioning of the space is just going to blow up in your face immediately". Katz (2024-01): at 1,536 dimensions exact search is "a million times 1,500 computations" and an index entry "must fit… within an 8 kilobyte limit within the page".

**Two properties that break database assumptions** (Vasnetsov, 2023): the result "actually depends on the order in which you insert", and "it's impossible to draw a clear line between relevant and irrelevant documents based solely on the vector similarity score". Christofides (2024-01): "your query result will not change as a result of you having an index. And that sentence is no longer true"; pgvector's README: "you will see different results for queries after adding an approximate index". **Recall is the measure** (Katz, 2024-11): eight of the exact ten is "80 %… recall is really the ultimate measurement of the quality of the system because… 10,000 queries per second… my recall is 20 % it's like so what". Liberty's dissent is the boundary with s7: "recall is actually a very poor surrogate of quality" of the *results* — recall@k measures the index against exact search; the s7 eval measures retrieval against the question.

**When no index is needed.** Eskildsen (2025-05): "If you have just a couple of thousand, tens of thousands or hundreds of thousands, I… just tell them to keep exhaustively search in memory because it's very simple and it's very easy to maintain. But as you get into the millions, you want to start thinking about building an approximate… index." Qdrant's course: "fewer than 10,000 vectors, brute force is often faster and uses less RAM than building HNSW"; Weaviate's dynamic index switches flat → HNSW at "by default 10,000" objects. **Position:** the walking skeleton runs exact search; an approximate index is a later, measured decision with a recorded threshold.

### 3.2 HNSW — mechanism, parameters, build, deletes

**Mechanism.** Dilocker (2023): a proximity graph, an entry point, "evaluate the edges… jump onto those new points", a visited list, stop "when we can't improve the scores anymore… roughly half of the graph is still blue. And that's our efficiency saving"; layers of fewer points with longer edges, searched top to bottom — "skip list is probably the better example". The abstract (Malkov and Yashunin): "The maximum layer in which an element is present is selected randomly with an exponentially decaying probability distribution… allows a logarithmic complexity scaling. Additional employment of a heuristic for selecting proximity graph neighbors significantly increases performance at high recall and in case of highly clustered data." Dilocker on that heuristic: pruning keeps only neighbours not reachable through another — "from now on, we're only going to do second-grade connections". Pinecone's Faiss chapter: level multiplier "1/ln(M)", "M_max0 set to M*2".

**The three parameters, said identically by four vendors.** `M`: "the max number of connections per layer (16 by default)" (pgvector); "between 8 and 64" (Qdrant course); Katz: "the most important parameter in terms of the quality of the index… 16 is kind of like a magic number". `ef_construction`: "the size of the dynamic candidate list for constructing the graph (64 by default)" (pgvector). `ef`: "top 10 results with an EF of 128" (Dilocker); `hnsw.ef_search` "40 by default" and "Results are limited by the size of the dynamic candidate list" (pgvector); Weaviate's dynamic `ef = min(max(dynamicEfMin, queryLimit * dynamicEfFactor), dynamicEfMax)`. Pinecone's Faiss measurements (Sift1M): recall "from 80%-1ms to 100%-50ms"; **"Both efConstruction and efSearch do not affect index memory usage, leaving us only with M"**. Katz's 0.8.0 cost model: `M` drives "the total number of tupal scanned" and "your embedding model though does impact how quickly you're able to… converge". Qdrant's presets (`m` 8/16/32, `ef_construct` 100/200/400, `hnsw_ef` 32/128/256) and Weaviate's recommended configurations are the practice's tuning table; Qdrant's two stated defaults are §4.

**Build.** Vasnetsov: an insert "is approximately two times more expensive than just searching", "very CPU intensified… a dedicated thread pool". Liberty (2021): "on a million point can easily run for an hour on a pretty beefy machine". pgvector: "Indexes build significantly faster when the graph fits into `maintenance_work_mem`"; "faster to create an index after loading your initial data". Weaviate (2025-02): "150 million Vector updates in a single day in a cluster… a lot of build cost". Random vectors make "a horrible graph" (Dilocker; Weaviate's FAQ agrees), duplicates too ("the early exit condition is never met" — Weaviate docs).

**Deletes.** Dilocker: a delete cannot remove the node ("I could risk my graph no longer being connected") — tombstone and rebuild "at… 30 % tombstones", or repair in the background, "a very costly process". Katz: "hnsw actually doesn't support a notion of deletion… you first hide it and then you repair the graph"; pgvector: "Vacuuming can take a while for HNSW indexes. Speed it up by reindexing first". Pinecone's guide, with a competing index: HNSW "does not do well with production applications with dynamic data" (§5). **Consequence:** the erasure proof of `data-ingestion/` Verify 7 queries; it does not count rows.

### 3.3 The other families and the storage medium each suits

Pinecone's guide (2024-05): hash-based (LSH) — "Almost no vector databases use hash-based indexing nowadays" (Liberty: it "doesn't use the statistics and the geometry of the given data"); tree-based "typically break down with very large vectors" (Katz); **clustered (IVF)** — centroids, search the nearest lists, "fewer, longer sequential reads", "little space overhead", "fast for writes", "lower query throughput than graph-based indexes in memory and on disk"; **graph** — "the fastest algorithms for in-memory vector search… They will not work in object storage"; **mixed** — "a graph on the centroids" over partitioned base data (SPANN; Weaviate's HFresh; turbopuffer's "clusters of clusters").

**IVFFlat in Postgres** (Katz; README): first "because it is a bit simpler to implement"; samples "about 10,000 vectors" for k-means; lists "rows / 1000 for up to 1M rows and sqrt(rows) for over 1M rows", probes "sqrt(lists)"; "Create the index after the table has some data"; probes scale "linearly"; "the centers start to skew" under inserts; fits "transient or ephemeral workloads". Christofides' analogy: the nearest capital, then the nearest town — "if something's close to a border… you don't necessarily get the right center".

**Clustered indexes for disk and object storage** (Eskildsen). A graph "is also not suitable for an object storage first or roundtrip sensitive database"; a clustered index needs "two round trips" — centroids, then the nearest clusters. turbopuffer's V1 rebuilt "the entire world"; V2 is incremental with **SPFresh** (split, merge, reassign — "a concurrency and implementation nightmare… but this works unbelievably well at very large scale"), **hierarchical clustering** at a billion vectors, and **RaBitQ** quantization with "a error bound on which ones we have to re-rank with the full fidelity vector". Weaviate's **HFresh** (docs, v1.36) is the same family: SPFresh posting lists on disk "compressed with 1-bit RQ", a centroid HNSW in memory at 8-bit RQ, "no full rebuilds", "not designed to beat HNSW on raw query throughput". DiskANN appears by name only (guide; ANN-Benchmarks: "Vamana, Vamana-PQ"). Qdrant's on-disk route is configuration: the `cold` memory tier, speed "depends on the disk's IOPS", optional `inline_storage` (v1.16).

### 3.4 Filtering — why it breaks the graph, and the four fixes

**The problem.** **Post-filtering** "risks to either turn the whole search into linear scan or return incomplete results" (Vasnetsov); Liberty: return "a thousand results… hope… at least 10… needless to say that's logically broken and oftentimes you're left with nothing"; the Qdrant presenter: "if the best match… isn't even in this top K, you would just end up not seeing it". **Pre-filtering** "nullifies the structure of the index" (Liberty), "requires a binary mask of the whole dataset… grows linearly" (Qdrant's benchmark page). **The reason** (Vasnetsov; his 2019 article): "percolation theory… 1 over K where K is the average number of connections per node… if we have 10 connections per node after removing 90 percent of nodes the graph will become disconnected… the accuracy drops almost almost to zero"; "this threshold can be shifted by increasing the m parameter".

**Fix 1 — filter-aware edges.** Vasnetsov: for declared payload fields, "build subgraph for each value of this field and then merge the subgraph… into the main one… our graph will always stay connected"; "the total complexity of the search is not affected"; ranges as "overlapping intervals", geo as geohashes; the article: "no more than 2 times" the edges. Dilocker (2025-02) for the correlated case — "gold ring… price filter for less than $10… either you lose the connectivity or you keep the connectivity but you keep scoring points that are never allowed by your filter… a variation of acorn". **Fix 2 — a planner by cardinality.** The Qdrant presenter: planning "per segment… based on filter cardinality, index availability and other thresholds… such as the full scan threshold" — broad: "regular HNSW search, skipping over the nodes that don't match"; narrow: "prefiltering using the payload indexes"; extra-narrow: "skip HNSW entirely and just use full scan"; the guide: "Our default full scan threshold is 10 kilobytes". ClickHouse: "decide whether to prefilter or post filter based on some heuristics", `vector_search_filter_strategy` overrides. pgvector 0.8.0 (release note): a corrected estimate "could lead PostgreSQL to select a B-tree or other index… 100 % recall". **Fix 3 — iterative scans (pgvector 0.8.0).** The README: **"With approximate indexes, filtering is applied *after* the index is scanned. If a condition matches 10 % of rows, with HNSW and the default `hnsw.ef_search` of 40, only 4 rows will match on average."** `hnsw.iterative_scan = strict_order | relaxed_order`, bounded by `hnsw.max_scan_tuples` ("20,000 by default"); "If filtering by only a few distinct values, consider partial indexing… many different values, consider partitioning." This refines the KB's RLS rule (§3.7). **Fix 4 — the payload index, declared early.** "Quadrant does not automatically index your payload fields… choose the field that restricts the search results the most" (presenter); "create payload indexes before uploading any data so HNSW can build filter-aware links" (course). Weaviate (Dilocker 2025-02): roaring bitmaps in the LSM store and a **bit-sliced index** for ranges — "you never have to merge more than 64 bit maps". Eskildsen: permissions are "very, very large intersects… well suited for… posting list". Qdrant's benchmark page names the outcomes under filters: "Speed boost", "Speed downturn", "Accuracy collapse… the HNSW graph becomes disconnected". Quantization and filtering are independent (Vasnetsov).

### 3.5 Quantization inside the store — SQ, BQ, PQ, RQ, and always rescoring

**Why.** Dilocker (2023): ada-002 at 1,536 float32 dimensions is "6 kilobytes… a million of them… 6 gigabytes… a billion… terabytes"; Weaviate's table: a node "4B (float) x N dimensions… 200-1200GB" at 100 M, edges "10B x 20 connections… 20GB". Katz: "random 4 byte floating point values… no way to compress them".

**Scalar (int8):** "a 75 % reduction in memory" (Qdrant presenter); "the go-to choice for most use cases… error rates remain below 1 %" (guide); Weaviate: "a 4x reduction". **Binary:** "a single bit which gives total of 32 times compression… two CPU instructions… bitwise [xor] and popcount" (Vasnetsov); "up to 40 times" faster, for "models that have at least 1,024 dimensions" (presenter); Weaviate: "encouraging recall with Cohere's V3 models… and OpenAI's ada-002… test BQ with your own data"; pgvector does it as an expression index (`binary_quantize(embedding)::bit(n)`, `bit_hamming_ops`) and its "Re-rank by the original vectors for better recall" query (`LIMIT 20` inside, `LIMIT 5` outside) is the smallest statement of the rescoring rule; Katz's chart: "33,000 queries per second… but it's only 80 % recall whereas the others… about 93 %". Radu (reused): "at scale, you will likely use binary with Hamming distance… float float only on the top end… on disk" — storage here, ranking s10. **Product:** Dilocker — codebooks of "256 options" per segment, "six or eight [dimensions per segment] works really great on something like OpenAI embeddings", accuracy "starts dropping very very rapidly" until you overfetch and rescore, then 24× compression at 95 % recall for "20 % or so throughput" (his SIFT chart); the codebook needs a representative sample, never "import it in order". Weaviate: segments "must divide evenly the original vector dimension", training "10,000 to 100,000 records per shard". Liberty (2021): the optimal centroid for a query distribution "should not be at the center at all". The presenter: PQ "we don't usually recommend due to the high accuracy loss", 4–64×. **Rotational (Weaviate):** "no training phase"; 8-bit "4x compression while maintaining 98-99 % recall in internal testing"; 4-bit (preview v1.39) "94-95 %… rescoring the top 50… 99.8 % or better"; 1-bit asymmetric "essentially matches BQ recall" on OpenAI embeddings and "works well… where BQ performs poorly"; "inspired by extended RaBitQ". Qdrant's `turbo4` and `turboquant` (v1.19) recorded by name.

**Rescoring and oversampling — every vendor.** Vasnetsov: "top 100 results we do rescoring using original representation… a parallel request to disk with asynchronous IO". Dilocker: rerank "within that top 128… quite close to the accuracy that you had before". Qdrant: `oversampling` then `rescore`; disabling it is "only applicable if using binary quantization or TurboQuant with 1, 1.5 and 2 bits (other methods do not rescore by default)". Weaviate: `rescoreLimit`; with 8-bit RQ "you can often disable rescoring". **Where the originals live — two vendors disagree.** Qdrant "will always maintain the original uncompressed vectors… you can safely experiment switching quantization… or even removing quantization entirely", but "Without explicitly setting on disk to true, you won't see any RAM savings"; Weaviate: "once set on a collection, quantization can't be disabled"; Eskildsen: "I have to store that in full precision cuz you want to be able to export that later… we don't support like int8 or binary vectors, but we will". **Position:** the store's quantization is a *second* quantization after s7's dtype ladder; the manifest records method, originals' placement, rescoring flag and before/after recall.

**The s7 parks, closed.** Johnson (MongoDB): quantization "at the model… But you can also do it inside the index" — this section. Multi-vector storage: Weaviate's table (8,000 token vectors × 768 float32 × 100,000 documents ≈ "~2.46 TB" vs "~4.9 GB" naive); ColPali "1,024 patches… Each gets a 128-dimensional embedding vector" per page; Eskildsen (2025-11): "very hard to earn a return on because it's an enormous amount of data… How much are you willing to pay for that extra 10 to 20 % of precision", "not seen anyone who's put it in prod yet"; Qdrant's course: "Disable HNSW for Reranking Embeddings". The Prompt Engineering VRAM figure found no second source.

### 3.6 Storage architecture — memory, disk, object storage, cold and warm

**The hierarchy and its prices** (Eskildsen; speaker's figures): RAM "about $5 per gigabyte"; three replicated SSDs "about 60 cents per gigabyte"; S3 "2 cents per gigabyte… 20 times cheaper"; a fully cached object-storage design "about 12 cents". NVMe is "maybe five times or four times slower than accessing memory if you use them correctly… about a 100x cheaper than DRAM"; a round trip "around 100 microseconds" against S3's "around 100 milliseconds" P99 per block. **Why vectors force it:** "a kilob of text… turns into… four vectors of… 1024 dimensions… 16 kilobytes of vectors from 1 kilobyte of text… storage amplification"; a bootstrapped company paying "3 to four grand a month" for Postgres was quoted "30 grand a month" to index its text "because the incumbent solutions were all doing all of this in memory". Dilocker (2023): "I only send like one query a day… separation of storage and compute… not solved yet"; Vasnetsov (2023): "making it cheap to serve billion vectors… you still need to have a lot of memory".

**Object-storage-native** (Eskildsen, 2026-03; his definitions). "Object storage is the only stateful dependency. There's no east-west coordination. There is no consensus layer in a separate system"; "coordination is done with compare and swap directly on object storage" — enabled by NVMe in clouds (2017), S3 strong consistency (2020), S3 compare-and-swap ("December of 2024"). A load balancer consistent-hashes the **namespace** ("a prefix on S3… a table or a tenant") to a query node; reads go memory → NVMe → object storage; writes go to a write-ahead log on S3 and are acknowledged only after S3 ("You could blow away every single VM… and we would not lose data"); autoscaled indexers compact in the background ("hundreds of thousands of nodes… back down to… tens" during re-ingestions "updating embedding models"). Strong consistency by default costs one metadata round trip ("about 8 milliseconds on S3 and about 15 milliseconds on Google Cloud Storage"). Costs he states: cold queries "500 to 1,000 milliseconds", writes "100 to 200 milliseconds" ("committing to object storage the new f-sync"), read-committed isolation. Rules: minimise round trips, maximise concurrency, large blocks, speculate, never `LIST` on a hot path. Scale figures and the "100 billion vectors with a P99 of 200 milliseconds" claim are §4.

**Hot, warm and cold in the incumbents.** Weaviate's vector cache ("If the cache fills, Weaviate drops the whole cache") and its 2024 "hot, warm, and cold storage tiers" (reused snapshot); Qdrant's `memory` tiers (`pinned`, `cached`, `cold`) and memmap; Liberty (2021): "degrade between those gracefully". **Position:** placement — RAM, SSD, object storage, what is pinned — is a manifest field with the cold- and write-latency budget it implies.

**The user's side** (Mickey Liu, 2025-05): "over 10 billion chunks… about 15 billion embeddings on a monthly basis at a peak of 100,000 embeddings per minute"; "about 100 QPS at peak"; recall tracked by "mean reciprocal rank and normalized discounted cumulative gain"; outcome "on track to save around a few million dollars annually… a p50 of 50 to 70 milliseconds". Eskildsen on what dominates: "it costs money to keep these indexes up to date… Lots of our customers do it close to every keystroke and it's generally driven by the economics of creating the embeddings", and "re-embedding everything to switch models like it's like it sucks. And so don't fall into that trap" — s7's pinning rule from the store.

### 3.7 Multi-tenancy at the index layer

**Four stores, four mechanisms.** *Qdrant*: one collection with a keyword payload index marked `is_tenant: true` — "co-locate vectors of the same tenant together" (guide); "Users very frequently make the mistake of creating a separate collection for each tenant… out-of-memory (OOM) errors" (filtering guide); Vasnetsov (2023): "build hsw graph only based on payload… only build subgraphs for the specific user IDs". *Weaviate*: the flat index "where each end user (i.e. tenant) has their own, isolated, dataset"; the dynamic index, "particularly useful in a multi-tenant setup", switches at 10,000 objects, one way; Dilocker 2023 did not reach his multi-tenancy section (§4). *turbopuffer*: "unlimited shards… every single piece of data that is logically divided… completely separated" — a namespace per Cursor codebase or Notion workspace, warmed "as soon as you open a codebase"; to "no natural partitions": "the only thing that scales even now is sharding… you want the shards to be as large as possible". *Postgres*: pgvector's README — **"sharing an approximate index between tenants means vectors from one tenant can affect recall (and speed) for other tenants. For tenant isolation, use list partitioning or separate tables."**

**What this does to the RLS rule.** Supabase (reused): "semantic search… will continue to respect these RLS policies… RLS is always applied even as new queries and application logic is introduced" — the s6 enforcement point stands. But the README says what RLS *is* to the index: a predicate "applied *after* the index is scanned", so a tenant owning 1 % of a shared HNSW gets, at `ef_search` 40, well under *k* results — recall loss that looks like an empty corpus. Supabase's example also lacks a `LIMIT` ("Combine with `ORDER BY` and `LIMIT` to use an index" — README). **Refinement (position):** RLS remains the control; recall needs one companion — `iterative_scan`, a partial index when tenants are few, list partitioning when many — and the two-tenant proof of `data-ingestion/` Verify 8 runs **with the approximate index present at the production `ef_search`** and asserts *k* results each, not only zero cross-tenant hits. Elsewhere the control is the store's partition or tenant index; the memory-store namespace rule of `15` §3.5 is the same mechanism.

### 3.8 Tuning, monitoring recall, capacity, the embedding call in the budget

**Latency versus throughput is a segment decision** (Vasnetsov; Qdrant's optimize page): one segment per core for latency, "fewer segments (usually 2) of larger size" for throughput. Qdrant's three recipes: quantized vectors in RAM with originals on disk; vectors and graph in the `cold` tier with `m` 64 and `ef_construct` 512; everything in RAM with quantization and rescoring. Weaviate: `vectorCacheMaxObjects` "high enough that all vectors can be held in memory" during import; async indexing on "a persistent, on-disk queue" (v1.28). **pgvector's list** (README): `maintenance_work_mem`, parallel workers, `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY`, `COPY … (FORMAT BINARY)`, indexes after the load, `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)`, `REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY` then `VACUUM`, `halfvec` for "a smaller working set", and the tuning order — **"use the defaults unless seeing low recall"**. Katz (2024): TOAST moves vectors over "2 kilobytes… around like 514 dimensions" out of line and "can screw up the query planner"; `SET STORAGE PLAIN` costs "a full page" per 1,536-dimensional vector; 8 KB index pages stop `vector` indexes at 2,000 dimensions (`halfvec` 4,000 — README); his `ef_construction` 256 is a speaker's recommendation against a default of 64.

**Monitoring recall.** Dilocker (2023): "occasionally do like sampled brute force comparisons and just see like does your recall drift over time"; pgvector gives the form — `SET LOCAL enable_indexscan = off` and compare. Eskildsen's **napkin math**: "construct a model, very simple model of the system… if that gap is off by an order of magnitude… either there's a massive opportunity to improve the system or there's a bug in your understanding" — "This always beats profiling." **Capacity.** The vendors' arithmetic agrees: vectors × dimensions × bytes, plus the graph ("10B x 20 connections" per node — Weaviate), plus replicas, plus the quantized copy when originals stay. Qdrant's page: "1M * 1536 * 4bytes * 1.5 = 8.6GB of RAM (including vectors + index)"; pgvector's reference: `4 * dimensions + 8` bytes (`vector`), `2 * dimensions + 8` (`halfvec`), `dimensions / 8 + 8` (`bit`). **Position:** the estimate is written before the build and is a spend line (T20). **The embedding call** (Eskildsen, 2025-11): "they're not thinking about the latency of the embedding model… The P50 latency you get is 300 milliseconds. Well, it doesn't matter that the turbopuffer latency is 8 milliseconds when it takes 300 milliseconds to create a query vector" — a criterion for `17` §3.6.

### 3.9 Choosing a store

**Search engine or database** (Vasnetsov, 2023). BASE against ACID — "think about postgres and elasticsearch"; "it should not be even used as a primary storage of data especially considering that the full update of vectors… due to… a new version of the model is just a common operation"; "if you are satisfied with scalability of postgres you can do that but… search engines are rarely the source of Truth". Eskildsen agrees on writes: turbopuffer is "at a read committed isolation level"; "you could never just like hot swap a relational database". **Postgres is enough until it is not.** Christofides: "the transactional nature… being able to join things together… lower maintenance overhead"; the README: 32 TB per table, WAL replication, Citus or PgDog; Eskildsen (2025-11): "you could probably get away with PG vector" for a small case; the reasons to leave are economics ("a 10x cheaper storage architecture") and operations ("hands-free… didn't need anyone… on call", Linear). Dilocker (2023): vendors sell HNSW "as a positive signal" or "as a negative… it's just engineering. There are trade-offs." **The OLAP option** (ClickHouse, 2026-01): a `vector_similarity` index "at the moment can only be hnsw", L2 or cosine, codec off, half a second cold. **Facts that decide (position, store-selection.md):** corpus size against the exact-search threshold; data and tenants already in Postgres (joins, RLS, one backup — the shape-A default); filter selectivity and tenant count; write rate and re-embedding cadence; QPS and latency budget including the embedding call; memory against the capacity estimate; cold-latency tolerance; who is on call. Rankings are not a fact.

### 3.10 Benchmarking at equal recall

**ANN-Benchmarks** (README; "no longer actively maintained… consider… VIBE"; results "as of April 2025"): "ground truth data for the top-100 nearest neighbors"; "Try many different values of parameters… ignore the points that are not on the precision-performance frontier"; "approximately 100-1000 dimensions"; "Single queries… only one CPU is saturated"; "Avoid extremely costly index building (more than several hours)"; "datasets that fit in RAM… big-ann-benchmarks" for billion scale. **The vendors' adaptations — method, not results.** Weaviate: "Recall@10 and Recall@100", multi-threaded QPS, mean and p99, import time; "Each request includes retrieving all the matched objects from disk… ann-benchmarks… only return the matched IDs"; "you cannot perform a single-threaded benchmark and extrapolate"; a debugging list (CPU architecture, random vectors, disk, the vector cache above 2 M). Qdrant: "two results must be compared only when you have similar precision… most benchmarks miss this critical aspect"; RPS versus latency as two scenarios; a memory cap "to ensure fairness"; filtered datasets "enriched with payload metadata and pre-generated filtering requests"; "Are we biased? Probably, yes." Katz: "measure performance and recall"; Liberty (2021): accuracy "depends on data". **Position:** the repo benchmarks its own corpus and queries — the s7 fixture as dataset, exact search over the same vectors as ground truth — recording recall@k, QPS at production concurrency, p99, memory and build time per configuration and date; comparisons only at equal recall; a vendor's page shortlists.

## 5. What is contested / trade-offs

- **HNSW versus clustered indexes.** Pinecone's guide (selling a partitioned serverless design): HNSW "does not do well with… dynamic data", "will not work in object storage". Weaviate: "150 million Vector updates in a single day" on HNSW, with HFresh beside it; Qdrant: HNSW with on-disk tiers; Eskildsen: graphs in memory, clusters for disk and object storage. **Position:** the index follows the placement — a graph in RAM for QPS over a bounded corpus; clustered or hybrid when the data lives on disk or object storage, changes constantly or is mostly cold.
- **Originals and reversibility.** Qdrant keeps originals and can switch quantization off; Weaviate makes it irreversible per collection; turbopuffer stores what you send. **Position:** record the originals' placement; a quantization change is a rebuild from stored originals, never a re-embed; an irreversible store gets the before/after first.
- **Pre-, post- or in-graph filtering.** The s5 hit-rate rule chooses between two; Qdrant and Weaviate add filter-aware edges and a cardinality planner; pgvector adds iteration; ClickHouse exposes the switch. **Position:** declare the strategy per filter class, let the planner choose where it can, measure filtered recall against exact; fewer than *k* results silently is the negative.
- **Tenant index versus partition per tenant.** Qdrant: not a collection per tenant (OOM); pgvector: not one shared approximate index (recall); Weaviate and turbopuffer partition. **Position:** store-shaped; the store-neutral part is the proof — two tenants, overlapping content, approximate index present, *k* results each, zero cross-tenant hits.
- **Search engine or system of record.** Vasnetsov: never the primary store; Katz and Christofides: keep it in Postgres. Compatible: Postgres *is* the record and may hold the index until §3.9's facts say otherwise; a dedicated engine is a derived index.
- **Recall as the metric.** Katz: "the ultimate measurement"; Liberty: "a very poor surrogate of quality". Different layers: recall@k measures the index, the s7 eval the retrieval, s5 the answer.
- **Who benefits.** Every speaker but Katz, Keen and the ANN-Benchmarks author sells the store; each vendor's benchmark page says the others are slower. Mechanisms kept when two agree; comparative claims in §4.

### 7.3 New principle — principles/18-vector-stores.md (to be created; draft until 2026-12-03; ≤ 28,000 bytes; vendor numbers by pointer only)

1. **The question** (≤ 0.4 KB). When a product retrieves by similarity over more vectors than it can scan: which index, with which parameters, under which filters, compressed how, placed where, isolated per tenant how, in which store, measured against what?
2. **Short answer** (≤ 1.7 KB). Exact search until a measured threshold; above it an approximate index whose recall@k against exact search is recorded with QPS, p99, memory and build time on the repo's own fixture, compared across configurations only at equal recall. HNSW in RAM for throughput over a bounded corpus; a clustered or hybrid index when the vectors live on disk or object storage, change constantly or are mostly cold. Filters break a graph past about 1/k of its nodes: declare the strategy per filter class and measure filtered recall; fewer than *k* silently is a bug. Store quantization is a second, recorded quantization with oversampling, rescoring and a before/after; know where the originals live and whether it reverses. Placement is a manifest field with the cold- and write-latency budget it implies. A shared approximate index leaks recall across tenants even behind RLS — partition, tenant index or namespace beside the control, proof at the production `ef`. The system of record stays the database; Postgres with pgvector is the default store until size, tenants, write rate, memory, latency including the embedding call, or operations say otherwise. Capacity is estimated before the build and is a spend line. Files: practices/vector-store/ (to be created).
3. **Long explanation** — §3.1 *Why exact search stops; when no index is needed* (≤ 2.4 KB); §3.2 *HNSW* (≤ 4 KB; defaults by pointer); §3.3 *Other families and media* (≤ 2.8 KB); §3.4 *Filtering: percolation and four fixes* (≤ 3.6 KB); §3.5 *Store quantization and rescoring* (≤ 3.2 KB; factors by pointer); §3.6 *Placement* (≤ 3.2 KB; prices as dated speaker statements, by pointer); §3.7 *Tenants and the RLS caveat* (≤ 2.4 KB); §3.8 *Tuning, monitoring, capacity, the embedding call* (≤ 2.4 KB); §3.9 *Choosing the store* (≤ 1.8 KB); §3.10 *Benchmarking at equal recall* (≤ 1.8 KB) — each mirrors the same-numbered section here.
4. **Apply in a repo** (≤ 1.4 KB) — the practice's Verify, in order. 5. **Anti-patterns** (≤ 1.2 KB) — an HNSW index at ten thousand vectors; parameters copied from a benchmark page; a shared approximate index behind RLS alone; a filtered query trusted to return *k*; a binary index without rescoring or a before/after; irreversible quantization set unmeasured; a graph on object storage; the vector store as system of record; a capacity estimate after the OOM; benchmarks compared at different recall; recall never sampled in production; the embedding call outside the latency budget. 6. **Evidence** (≤ 1.6 KB). 7. **Change log** (≤ 0.3 KB).

### 7.4 Glossary entries (`principles/00-glossary.md` — already 32 KB: six new entries of three lines at most; the existing *Pre-filter vs post-filter authorisation (RAG)* and *Row Level Security (RLS)* entries each gain one clause instead of a new entry)

- **Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) index; recall@k.** An index returning neighbours that are probably, not certainly, the closest, trading recall for speed and memory; results change on rebuild and differ from exact search; recall@k is the share of the exact top-k returned. Vasnetsov (2023); Katz (2024-11); pgvector README (read 2026-10-05). `18` §3.1.
- **HNSW (`M`, `ef_construction`, `ef`).** A layered proximity graph searched greedily from a sparse top layer; `M` edges per node (memory, recall), `ef_construction` the build beam (quality, build time), `ef` the query beam (recall, latency). Malkov and Yashunin (2016–18); Dilocker (2023). `18` §3.2.
- **Clustered (IVF) index; flat index; dynamic index.** Centroids from k-means and a search of the nearest lists — fewer, longer reads, suited to disk and object storage; flat is exact search; dynamic starts flat and becomes HNSW past a threshold. Katz (2024-01); Pinecone guide (2024-05); Weaviate docs (read 2026-10-05). `18` §3.1, §3.3.
- **Filterable HNSW; percolation threshold; iterative scan.** Filtering a graph in place disconnects it past about 1/k of its nodes; fixes are extra edges per payload value, a planner switching by cardinality, or scanning until *k* results pass. Vasnetsov (2019, 2023); pgvector 0.8.0 (2024-11). `18` §3.4.
- **Store quantization (SQ, BQ, PQ, RQ); oversampling and rescoring.** Compressing stored vectors for memory and speed, then re-ranking an oversampled candidate list against the originals. Dilocker (2023); Qdrant and Weaviate docs (read 2026-10-05). `18` §3.5.
- **Object-storage-native store; placement (hot / warm / cold).** A store whose only stateful dependency is object storage, coordinated by compare-and-swap, with caches warming hot namespaces into SSD and RAM; placement names where vectors and index live and what cold latency costs. Eskildsen (2026-03). `18` §3.6.
- *Pre-filter vs post-filter authorisation* gains "on an approximate index post-filtering loses recall — see *filterable HNSW*"; *RLS* gains "a post-scan predicate on pgvector's HNSW: partition or iterative scan beside it (`18` §3.7)".
