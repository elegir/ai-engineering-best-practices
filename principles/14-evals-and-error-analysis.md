# Evals and error analysis

## 1. The question this answers

How do we know an LLM feature works, keep knowing after every change to a prompt, a model, a tool or a schema, and improve it on purpose — without buying a score we do not understand?

## 2. Short answer

An eval is **applied data science on your own product**, not a test suite you buy. It starts with a **domain owner reading traces**, one specific note per trace about the most upstream error (*open coding*); the notes are clustered into named **failure modes** and counted (*axial coding*); only persistent, non-obvious failure modes get a **grader** — code where code can decide, an LLM judge where it cannot — and every grader is **binary and one per failure mode**. A judge is a **classifier**: calibrated against the owner's labels on a held-out split with true-positive and true-negative rates (or Cohen's κ) and the confusion matrix, **never raw agreement**, then pinned (model and prompt version) and re-calibrated when either changes. The **suite** is 20–50 tasks from real failures, each with a reference solution that passes, each trial from a clean environment, graded on the **outcome, not the path**, run k times and reported as **pass^k** (all k pass) with the worst case shown. Judges **gate every change** beside deterministic graders and grade a **production sample** on a cadence whose rate was raised until the series stabilised. The human owns taxonomy and labels; the agent builds the tooling, maintains the taxonomy and proposes instances of known failure modes — never "evaluate my app". A tool's default 0–1 score at 0.5 is someone else's judge prompt: the tool is runner and datastore; the graders and the calibration record are yours. The retrieval stage gets its own deterministic eval before the application's (§3.3, s7).

## 3. Long explanation

### 3.1 Lifecycle and error analysis — analyze → measure → improve

**What an eval is.** Hamel Husain (Lenny's Podcast, 2025-09, `podcast-BsWxPI9UM4c…`): "a way to systematically measure and improve an AI application… at its core data analytics on your LLM application". Shreya Shankar widens it: unit tests are "a very small part of that very big puzzle"; evals include looking at data, product metrics, monitoring and A/B tests. Eugene Yan (`eugeneyan-eval-process.md`, 2025-04): "building product evals is simply the scientific method in disguise" — observe, annotate a balanced set, hypothesise, experiment, measure — and **eval-driven development**: "first, write some evals; then, build systems that pass those evals". Shankar's lifecycle (`yt-tqUDjc1HzO4…`, 2026-07): **analyze** (find the failure modes), **measure** (graders), **improve** (fix and re-measure); AI is weak at the first and useful at the other two (§3.4).

**Error analysis before any grader.** Husain's live walk-through (Lenny's, on Nurture Boss, an apartment-leasing assistant): open a trace, read it "with your product hat on", write one short note — the **most upstream error only**, specific enough to categorise later ("did not confirm call transfer with user", never "janky"). This is **open coding**, a social-science term the model also knows. "At least a hundred" traces is a mental unblock; the real stop is **theoretical saturation** — new traces stop producing new kinds of note. Then **axial coding**: hand the notes to a model using the words "open codes" and "axial codes", rename the generic categories ("capability limitations… that's not actionable"), map every note with a prompt that includes **"none of the above"**, and **count** — "basic counting is the most powerful analytical technique in data science". Shankar adds the Pareto shape ("80 % of issues… caused by 20 % of failure modes") and the loop: after each new label the agent re-applies it to the traces already reviewed.

**One owner — the benevolent dictator.** One person with domain taste reads and decides ("for legal… a law person… oftentimes it is the product manager" — Husain), because committees make it "so expensive that you can't do it". Lucas of Nova Escola, a Brazilian lesson-plan generator (`yt-mF4CaijvJos…`, 2026-07), supplies the negative: two annotators who "disagreed more than if I was flipping a coin" were "a sign that the rubric wasn't clear", at "a month of work" per relabelling round. Ng said it in 2022 (`yt-avoijDORAlc…`): inconsistent labels mean "I didn't write clear enough labeling instructions" — hence the **label book** and **agreement-based labeling** (`16-data-for-ai-products.md` §3.2).

**Criteria drift.** Yan's blog (`eugeneyan-llm-evaluators.md`, 2024-08) summarising Shankar et al.'s *Who validates the validators* (2024): "it is impossible to completely determine evaluation criteria prior to human judging of LLM outputs"; and Yan recounting a participant who asked to "enforce that we look at at least 20 examples first" (`yt-7EGF0Mc0_os…`, 2024-08). Yan flipped his own workflow — the human reads "at least 20 or even 50" input–output pairs before writing criteria, which otherwise come "from an ivory tower". "Evals are the new PRDs" (Lenny) holds only if the judge prompt is derived from the data and keeps changing.

**Not every failure needs an eval.** "If it's obvious, do it… just fix your application" (Husain); judges only for "the pesky ones" — "between four and seven" per product (Shankar). Lucas's first attempt labelled against a full pedagogical rubric and "spent a lot of time labeling data that we didn't need": most fixes were one prompt change. The decision column of `practices/evals/error-analysis-log.md` (fix / code grader / judge / ignore) is this rule as a file.

### 3.2 Graders and judges — binary, calibrated, pinned

**Three kinds of grader** (`anthropic-demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents.md`, 2026-01-09): **code-based** (string and regex matches, fail-to-pass tests, state checks — fast, objective, "brittle to valid variations"); **model-based** (rubrics, pairwise, reference-based — "requires calibration with human graders"); **human** (expert review, spot checks — the gold standard, expensive): "deterministic graders where possible, LLM graders where necessary… human graders judiciously". Husain's three levels (`hamel-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md`, 2024) are the same ladder by cadence: assertions on every change, human and model evaluation on a cadence, A/B tests. Shankar: code for a format or a JSON shape; a judge when "we don't know how to evaluate in an automated way".

**One failure mode, one judge, binary.** Husain: a 1–5 scale "in most cases… is a weasel way of not making a decision… no one knows what 3.2 versus 3.7 means"; Shankar: "you should be anti-Likert-scale LLM judge". The judge is narrow — "evaluate one failure mode… pass or fail" — which is why a judge is easier to write than the product. Yan from the literature: "simplifying to binary actually leads to better performance"; for *subjective* criteria **pairwise comparison** is more reliable, while objective ones (faithfulness, policy) need direct scoring — "the better option from a pair might still be a defect". Anthropic's agents post converges: "grade each dimension with an isolated LLM-as-judge" and "give the LLM a way out… return 'Unknown'". This refines the s4 rule of ≤ 5 classes (`13-structured-outputs-and-guardrails.md` §3.4): a *grader* is binary per failure mode; five classes only for a *graded decision*.

**Reason first, then discard.** Yan: without a reasoning step the judge was "just not usable"; with it, "going from not usable to usable" (Liu's 2024 figures stay in the digest §4). Anthropic's docs page (`anthropic-develop-tests.md`, read 2026-10-01): "reason first… and then discard the reasoning". Husain's 2024 spreadsheet already had a *critique* column beside the binary outcome — the critique is what lets you fix the judge prompt. Confirms s4 (`13` §3.4; `05-verification-loops.md`).

**Calibration: measure the judge like a classifier.** Husain: once the prompt exists "a lot of people stop there… Don't do that". Measure it against the labels from axial coding and **never report raw agreement**: "if you only have the error 10 % of the time, then you can easily have 90 % agreement by just having a judge say it passes all the time". Look at the two off-diagonal cells of the confusion matrix and iterate until both are small; agreement without the matrix is "a bad smell". Yan: **Cohen's κ** corrects for chance (80 % agreement was κ 0.62 in his example); correlation metrics are "a fairly bad smell" because they do not translate to production recall. Lucas adds the hygiene: a **training split** for prompt iteration and a **held-out split** for the number, balanced sets, and a **pinned model**: "every time we run again the judge, we use exactly the same model, so we can say that the TPR and TNR still applies". Anthropic agrees: "closely calibrated with human experts". The record (set, TPR, TNR, κ, matrix, model, version, date) lives in the judge file's header (`practices/evals/judge-prompt-template.md`) and `practices/evals/eval_harness.py --calibrate` computes it by hand.

**Known judge biases** (Yan's survey of 2023–24 papers, `eugeneyan-llm-evaluators.md`): **position bias** in pairwise comparison, **verbosity bias**, **self-enhancement bias**; strong models "over-reasoning" on reference matching until told "don't overthink". Fine-tuned judges "essentially functioned as task-specific classifiers"; a **panel of smaller judges** beat one large judge at a fraction of the cost. Biases durable, magnitudes history (digest §4).

**Evaluator or guardrail.** Yan's decision tree: as an *evaluator* on a few hundred samples offline, an LLM API with reasoning and n-shot examples; as a *guardrail* in production, "invest in finetuning a classifier or reward model, bootstrapping it on… labels you've collected during internal evals" — the tiers of `practices/structured-outputs/guardrail-tiers.md` seen from the eval side. Chip Huyen (`podcast-mrYn6_6gJuY…`, 2024-12): a RAG hallucination is a **factual inconsistency with the retrieved context**, a natural-language-inference question, so an NLI classifier is the right grader (Galileo's Luna claim is the vendor's).

**Own your judge prompts.** Huyen's "number one mistake" is undefined criteria used as exact — "coherence, relevancy… very highly dependent on the model you use, the prompt for the judge"; judge prompts in popular open-source tools "almost every single one have a little typo", and fixing one silently changes every score. With Lucas's pinned model, the rule: **a judge prompt is a versioned artifact with a calibration record attached** — the eval-side twin of `10-llm-api-fundamentals.md` §3.3.

**The Likert dissent.** Anthropic's older docs page (`anthropic-develop-tests.md`, read 2026-10-01) shows 1–5 Likert and ordinal graders and says "prioritize volume over quality". With the lifecycle explicit, the page describes *measure* and Husain *analyze* (digest §5). **Position (opinion):** binary per failure mode with the owner's labels first; many automated graders after; a 1–5 grade only where a graded decision is genuinely needed and then calibrated like any other score.

### 3.3 Suites, harnesses and numbers — Anthropic's roadmap and pass^k

**Vocabulary** (`anthropic-demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents.md`): a **task** (inputs and success criteria), a **trial** (one attempt; run several), a **grader** (logic scoring one aspect; several per task), the **transcript** (the full record, tool calls included), the **outcome** (the final environment state — "whether a reservation exists in the environment's SQL database", not that the agent said "booked"), the **eval harness** (runs, records, grades, aggregates — distinct from the agent harness of `02-harness-engineering.md`), the **suite**. Glossary: `00-glossary.md`.

**From zero to a suite**, first person: **start early** — "20–50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start"; "evals get harder to build the longer you wait"; start with what you already test manually, the bug tracker and the support queue. **Unambiguous tasks with a reference solution** — "two domain experts would independently reach the same pass/fail verdict"; "a 0 % pass rate… is most often a signal of a broken task". **Balance the set** — the web-search eval needed should-search and should-answer queries to stop over- and under-triggering. **Isolate each trial** — shared state causes correlated failures ("the git history from previous trials"). **Grade what the agent produced, not the path** — exact tool sequences give "overly brittle tests"; **partial credit** on multi-part tasks; **hack-resistant graders**. **Read the transcripts** — "we do not take eval scores at face value until someone… reads some transcripts" (CORE-Bench went from 42 % to 95 % once grading and specs were fixed). **Watch for saturation** — a suite at 100 % "provides no signal". Two kinds: **capability evals** that "should start at a low pass rate" and **regression evals** held near 100 %; a capability eval graduates once reliably passed.

**pass@k and pass^k.** *pass@k* — at least one success in k trials — rises with k; *pass^k* — **all** k succeed — falls: "a 75 % per-trial success rate… 3 trials… (0.75)³ ≈ 42 %", the metric "for customer-facing agents where users expect reliable behavior every time". This names what Dan Klein asked for (`podcast-2HwtyPE6JuQ…`, 2026-04, reused from s1): "for how many of these scenarios will I get it right every time, 100 times in a row" — the s1 rule "run N times, read the worst case" is pass^k reported per scenario (`10` §3.1; `practices/llm-api-calls/failure-modes-and-mitigations.md` row 12). Uber's pass@K for its retry loop is the other reading: one success in K suffices because deterministic gates follow (§3.4).

**Layers and cadence.** Anthropic lists automated evals, production monitoring, A/B, user feedback, weekly transcript review and human studies as Swiss-cheese layers — "triage feedback constantly, sample transcripts to read weekly". The operating rule from production (Lucas): judges run **as a CI gate on every PR** and **on a daily sample of production**; a 2 % sample gave "very erratic" numbers, so he raised the rate until the series stabilised ("around 80 % in my case"). Shankar's online judges "sample a thousand traces every day". Confirms the s3 rule: offline evals are unit tests, online evals are monitoring (`05`, 2026-09-30). Table: `practices/evals/eval-policy.md` §4. Evals per pipeline stage (the parser on thousands of the product's own pages), the production sample's reason for RAG, and lineage on the golden set are `16-data-for-ai-products.md` §3.2 and `eval-policy.md` §3 (s6). **The retrieval stage has its own eval** (s7, `17-embeddings-and-chunking.md` §3.3): synthetic questions with verbatim excerpts, graded on token-level recall, precision and IoU (Chroma, 2024-07-03); the recall-of-the-source-chunk loop — generate a question from a chunk, check that the chunk comes back (Jason Liu, TWIML 2024-11; `jxnl-low-hanging-fruit-rag-search.md`); Glean's unit tests per model behaviour (2025-03); Radu Gheorghe's nDCG blind spot — unjudged results count as zero, so re-judge after every relevance change (Vespa, 2026-08). Deterministic, no judge; harness in `practices/embeddings-and-chunking/chunk-eval-harness.md`.

### 3.4 Automation and the production loop

**What AI can and cannot do** (Shankar, `yt-tqUDjc1HzO4…`). The argument is epistemic: "if a tool could fully build and fix your product for you, it could do the same for… all of your competitors… your judgment is the only differentiating factor"; "what good means for your product is living in your head, it's not in the traces". Three mistakes: (1) "check out my traces, evaluate the app" to a coding agent — it "will miss all of the taste-specific issues", "fabricates what they think is the highest priority", and leaves **no reusable intermediate** (failure-mode definitions must be persisted); (2) reviewing each trace once; (3) "a uniform accuracy bar for all of your AI applications" — set the bar by **worst-case reasoning** from the user's shoes. What she automates: the agent reads a sample, **builds a review app** (trace viewer, cluster map, progress view), samples per cluster, then **monitors her in-situ annotations, maintains the taxonomy and proposes instances of a known failure mode** in other traces — "I don't tell the agent to find me entirely new examples of failure modes". Husain: the published skills "are meant to be customized… ultimately it's somebody else's prompt". Brief: `practices/evals/annotation-ui-brief.md`.

**Remove the friction from looking at data.** Husain (2024): "build my own data viewing & labeling tool so I can gather all the information I need onto one screen" — Gradio, Streamlit, Shiny, "in less than a day". *Paired error analysis* (`yt-FzO7NRe_7VA…`, 2026-04) shows a developer and a domain expert building the annotation app together from a raw JSONL export: "when you explore the data, you come up with some hypotheses". DoorDash (`yt-bMjlRrWjdT0…`, 2026-08): UI-first for non-engineers, then **API-first** ("very stable APIs" for traces, scores, datasets) so that "strat-ops teams… vibe-code their own annotation UIs"; its loop is tracing → sampling → annotation → review → golden datasets → judge calibration (original and optimised judge prompt side by side) → monitoring.

**Closing the loop without a human** (Uber, `yt-31GUkCBD-Uc…`, 2026-07) — the module's one fully closed loop, kept as an **at-scale pattern with preconditions**. Logging first: one flat JSON per orchestration. The **router** is evaluated as a classifier with a confusion matrix, its guardrail metric **recall**. **Human labels are the golden source of truth**, on a representative cut with "a very objective guideline". Drift: sample production, send to the same labelers, compare; on mismatch a diagnosis agent localises the failing step and triggers **auto-tuning** (*reflect* and *synthesize* sub-agents rewriting the config) — **benchmarked against the golden set and registered only if it passes**, "quick rollback built in". The enhancement loop is generate → QA gate → regenerate-with-feedback (pass@K), then a second "publish-ready" QA with an *unsure* verdict that rejects. **Position (opinion):** close the loop only after a golden set exists and only for changes measured against it; the human moves to labelling and rollback; a judge still never gates a publish alone (`05`). Prompt optimisation itself (GEPA, DSPy, Uber's optimiser) is parked for session 16.

### 3.5 Tooling and ownership

**Tools are runner and datastore, not judge.** promptfoo (`promptfoo-intro.md`, read 2026-10-01) is a declarative runner with matrix views, caching, CI and red-teaming, local by default. DeepEval (`deepeval-getting-started.md`, read 2026-10-01) is a pytest plugin whose first example scores a criteria string 0–1 against a default threshold, and whose second grades with the Jev decision model (re-evaluate after 2026-12). Anthropic lists Harbor, Braintrust, LangSmith, Langfuse and Phoenix: frameworks "are only as good as the eval tasks you run through them". **The KB's reading**, with Lucas's complaint ("they assume your judge is calibrated, so they go straight to give you a score. And I don't want a score") and Huyen's typos beside the defaults: **a tool's default score and threshold are someone else's judge prompt and cut-off; a 0–1 score at a default threshold is a Likert scale in disguise until calibrated** — use the tool as **runner and datastore**, write your own binary graders, keep the calibration record outside it. Lucas's stack is one worked shape: Langfuse as traces and datastore, Claude Code with Husain's skills over the Langfuse MCP, four judges. Shapes, versions and defaults, dated: `practices/evals/stack-notes/` and `practices/evals/eval-policy.md` §2.

**Ownership is cross-functional.** DoorDash: ops set the bar, product writes rubrics, ops annotate, engineering provides APIs and judges. Anthropic: "dedicated evals teams… own the core infrastructure, while domain experts and product teams contribute most eval tasks"; PMs can contribute an eval task as a PR — "let them". This closes the s12 park on Notion's three tiers (CI regression, a launch report card per journey, "frontier headroom" evals — `05` change log 2026-09-24): the regression / capability split with a per-journey report, owned the same way. The Amplify survey (`yt-RGe6EjucbzI…`, 2026-07, self-selected) is context only: "evals" the top stack challenge "every single year".

## 4. How to apply it in a repo

This is the Verify of `practices/evals/README.md`, in order; the practice is `draft` and **unrouted** until the routing gate of `decisions/0004-day-one-for-blank-and-existing-repos.md` §6.

1. Confirm `llm_calls` (`practices/facts.md`); name the **one owner** of labels and rubrics in `docs/eval-policy.md` §1.
2. Export traces; the owner open-codes at least twenty (one most-upstream note each) into `evals/error-analysis.md` before any criterion is written; cluster, count, keep "none of the above"; decide fix / code grader / judge / ignore per failure mode.
3. Build the regression suite as data: `evals/tasks.jsonl` with ≥ 20 tasks from real failures, each with a reference solution; a clean environment per trial; graders on the outcome or output, partial credit on multi-part tasks (`eval_harness.py`).
4. One judge file per judged failure mode from `judge-prompt-template.md`: definition from the log, pass/fail criteria, two contrasting examples, reasoning before verdict, `unknown`; **calibrate** on a held-out labelled split with `--calibrate`; record TPR, TNR, κ, matrix, model, version in the header; pin the model.
5. Run k ≥ 5 trials per task; report pass^k and the worst case; the floor and the gate in `eval-policy.md` §3–4; CI on every change to a prompt, schema, tool set, model, judge or policy.
6. First-user: a production sample graded on the cadence, rate raised until stable, series recorded; one person reads transcripts weekly; failures readable in the review app built from `annotation-ui-brief.md`.
7. Tools (promptfoo, DeepEval, Langfuse, LangSmith) as runner and datastore; library thresholds disabled or calibrated; records in the repo.

## 5. Anti-patterns

- Judge prompts written before anyone read the traces; criteria "from an ivory tower".
- A committee labelling; annotators below coin-flip agreement treated as a vote, not as an unclear rubric.
- A 1–10 score averaged into "3.7"; a tool's 0–1 at 0.5 shipped as a gate.
- Agreement reported as calibration; a judge calibrated on the split it was tuned on; an unbalanced labelled set.
- An unpinned judge model; a judge prompt edited with no version bump or re-calibration.
- A mean over flaky scenarios; pass@k reported where pass^k was the question.
- Grading the exact tool sequence; a task at 0 % with no reference solution; shared state between trials.
- A judge gating a send, payment or publish alone (`05`).
- "Evaluate my app" to a coding agent; an agent adding failure modes by itself; no persisted definitions.
- No production sample; a dashboard number nobody traced back to a transcript in a month.
